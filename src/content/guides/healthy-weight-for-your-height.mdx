"What should I weigh?" is one of the most common health questions — and one of the most misunderstood. There is no single perfect number, but there are useful reference points. This guide explains the classic ideal-weight formulas, how to read them, and why a *range* is the honest answer.

## Where the formulas come from

The calculator shows four well-known equations — **Robinson, Miller, Devine and Hamwi** — each developed last century, several originally for calculating medication doses. They all estimate a reference weight from your height and sex, adding a fixed amount of weight for every inch above five feet. Because they were derived differently, they disagree by a few pounds, which is exactly why you should treat the results as a range.

## Ideal weight vs. healthy weight

"Ideal body weight" and "healthy weight" aren't the same thing. The formulas give a single tidy figure, but real bodies vary. That's why the calculator also shows the weight range that corresponds to a **healthy BMI (18.5–24.9)** for your height — usually a more realistic target band than any one formula.

## What the formulas miss

Height and sex alone can't capture everything:

- **Muscle mass** — athletes often weigh more than the formulas suggest while being very lean.
- **Frame size** — a larger skeletal frame naturally carries more weight.
- **Body composition** — where and how you carry weight matters more than the number itself.

For that reason, pair this with the [BMI calculator](/health/bmi-calculator) and the [body fat calculator](/health/body-fat-calculator) for a fuller picture.

## The takeaway

Aim for the range, not a magic number, and weigh trends over time rather than daily fluctuations. Use the [ideal weight calculator](/health/ideal-weight-calculator) as a starting point for a conversation with a healthcare professional — not as a verdict.
